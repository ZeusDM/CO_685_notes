\section*{Picking the Group and Representation for Diffie--Hellman}

In Diffie--Hellman key exchange, parties choose a public cyclic group \(G = \langle g \rangle\) of order \(n\), sample private exponents \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}_n\), and exchange \(g^a, g^b\) to establish the shared secret \(g^{ab}\).
Every cyclic group of order \(n\) is abstractly isomorphic to \((\mathbb{Z}_n, +)\).
However, \emph{the concrete representation of group elements dictates both the computational efficiency for honest users and the security against discrete logarithm attacks}.
To achieve 128-bit security, every attack must require at least \(\approx 2^{128}\) operations, while honest parties want exponentiation via square-and-multiply to be as fast as possible.

\paragraph*{Approach 1: Pick a random 3072-bit prime \(p\) and use \(\mathbb{Z}_p^\times\)}

To resist the subexponential GNFS attack (\(L_p[1/3, 1.923] \approx 2^{128}\)), the modulus \(p\) must have at least \(3072\) bits.
However, the group order is \(p - 1\), which is composite.
Picking a random \(3072\)-bit prime \(p\) gives no guarantee that \(p - 1\) has large prime factors.
If \(p - 1\) happens to be smooth (i.e., its largest prime factor \(p_{\max}\) is small), Pohlig--Hellman solves the discrete logarithm problem in polynomial time.

\paragraph*{Approach 2: Safe primes (\(p = 2q + 1\))}

To defeat Pohlig--Hellman, we can choose a \(3072\)-bit safe prime \(p = 2q + 1\) where \(q\) is also prime (a Sophie Germain prime), so that \(p - 1 = 2q\).
We work in the unique subgroup of prime order \(q\) (the quadratic residues), generated by \(g \equiv x^2 \pmod p\) for \(x \not\equiv \pm 1 \pmod p\).

However, the group order \(q \approx 2^{3071}\) is unnecessarily large: exponents have \(\approx 3072\) bits, forcing square-and-multiply to perform \(\approx 3072\) modular multiplications.
With each multiplication costing \(O((\log p)^2)\), exponentiation has bit complexity \(O((\log p)^3)\).
Achieving \(O(\sqrt{q}) \approx 2^{1536}\) security against Pollard's \(\rho\) is massive overkill when GNFS already caps overall security at \(2^{128}\); we are performing \(3072\) modular multiplications when only \(256\) would suffice for generic security.

\paragraph*{Approach 3: Schnorr groups (\(p = fq + 1\))}

We can decouple the subgroup order from the field size by picking a \(256\)-bit prime \(q\) and a \(3072\)-bit prime \(p = fq + 1\) for an integer cofactor \(f \approx 2^{2816}\).
Setting \(g \equiv x^f \pmod p\) (for \(x^f \not\equiv 1\)) generates a subgroup of prime order \(q\).
Because exponents are only \(256\) bits, exponentiation requires only \(\approx 256\) multiplications modulo \(p\), running in \(O(\log q \cdot (\log p)^2)\) bit operations---roughly \(12\) times faster than safe primes.

An attack using Pollard's \(\rho\) on the subgroup of order \(q\) would require \(O(\sqrt{q}) \approx 2^{128}\) operations, which is cryptographically large.
Furthermore, an attack using GNFS or index calculus must work in the ambient multiplicative group modulo the larger \(p\)---even though all protocol elements lie in the subgroup of order \(q\)---requiring \(L_p[1/3, 1.923] \approx 2^{128}\) operations, which is also cryptographically large.

\paragraph*{Approach 4: Polynomial rings and extension fields}

Another idea is to work in a finite field extension \(\mathbb{F}_q = \mathbb{F}_{p^d} \cong \mathbb{F}_p[x] / (f(x))\), where \(f(x) \in \mathbb{F}_p[x]\) is an irreducible polynomial of degree \(d\).
In fact, all finite fields can be formed this way, and the multiplicative group \(\mathbb{F}_q^\times\) is cyclic of order \(p^d - 1\).

To achieve the same representation size as a prime field \(\mathbb{Z}_{p'}\), we need \(d \log p \approx \log p'\).
However, discrete logarithm in \(\mathbb{F}_{p^d}\) is strictly easier than in \(\mathbb{Z}_{p'}\): for fixed small \(p\) (such as characteristic \(2\)), the best attack \cite{GrangerKleinjungZumbragel2018} runs in \emph{quasipolynomial time} in \(d\):
\[
    \exp\left( \left(\frac{1}{\log 2} + o(1)\right) \log^2 d \right).
\]
Hence, finite fields of small characteristic cannot provide adequate security.

\paragraph*{Approach 5: Elliptic curves}

Approach~3 seems to be the best among these options, but it still has the significant drawback of living in this much larger group unnecessarily: working in a subgroup of \(\mathbb{Z}_p^\times\) forces us to perform operations on \(3072\)-bit integers.
Elliptic curves resolve this dilemma.

\section*{Elliptic curves}

\begin{definition}[Elliptic curve in characteristic not 2 or 3]
    An elliptic curve over a field \(K\) of characteristic not 2 or 3 is an equation of the form
    \[
        y^2 = x^3 + ax + b
    \]
    for some \(a, b \in K\) such that \(4a^3 + 27b^2 \neq 0\).
    We also identify an elliptic curve with the set of points \((x, y) \in K^2\) satisfying its equation, together with a point \(\infty\) at infinity.
\end{definition}

A more general equation (the generalized Weierstrass equation) can be defined for fields of characteristic 2 or 3; we will introduce those if needed later.

To turn the set of points on an elliptic curve into a commutative group, we need to define an operation, denoted additively by \(+\).

Consider any non-vertical line \(y = mx + q\).
Finding its intersection with the elliptic curve \(y^2 = x^3 + ax + b\) requires computing the roots of the degree 3 polynomial \((mx + q)^2 = x^3 + ax + b\).
A key fact is that if this cubic polynomial has two roots, it must have a third root.
Counting multiplicity of roots, if a line intersects the curve in at least two points, it intersects in three points (say \(P, Q, R\)).
For these three points, we require
\[
    P + Q + R = \infty,
\]
taking the point at infinity \(\infty\) to be the identity.

Vertical lines \(x = c\) can intersect the curve in two points without having a third.
We fix this by defining that \(\infty\) lies on every vertical line.
Thus, a vertical line through a point \(P\) and its reflection \(P'\) also passes through \(\infty\), giving \(P + P' + \infty = \infty\), which implies \(P' = -P\).
Rearranging \(P + Q + R = \infty\) then gives \(P + Q = -R = R'\), the reflection of \(R\).

\begin{definition}[Group operation on an elliptic curve]
    Given two points \(P, Q\) on an elliptic curve, let \(\ell\) be the unique line passing through \(P\) and \(Q\) (or the tangent line at \(P\) if \(P = Q\)).
    Since \(\ell\) intersects the curve in at least two points counting multiplicity, it must intersect in a third point \(R\) (which could be \(\infty\) if \(\ell\) is vertical, or one of \(P\) or \(Q\) if that point has higher multiplicity).
    We define \(P + Q\) as the reflection of \(R\) across the \(x\)-axis.
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Vertical tangent: \(P + P = \infty\)]
    When the tangent line at \(P\) is vertical (as in \Cref{fig:ecvert}), it intersects the curve at \(P\) with multiplicity 2 and has no other affine intersection points.
    Its third intersection point is the point at infinity, \(R = \infty\), which gives
    \[
        P + P = \infty.
    \]
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Inflection point: \(P + P = -P\)]
    When \(P\) is an inflection point (as in \Cref{fig:ecinflect}), the tangent line at \(P\) crosses through the curve and meets it at \(P\) with multiplicity 3.
    Thus, the third intersection point is \(R = P\) itself, which gives
    \[
        P + P = -P \quad (\text{or } 3P = \infty).
    \]
\end{example}
