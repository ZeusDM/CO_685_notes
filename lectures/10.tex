\lecture{10}{2026-10-20}{Elliptic Curves, Algebraically}

In the previous lecture, we introduced elliptic curves and defined the group operation geometrically using the chord-and-tangent construction.
In this lecture, we translate this geometric picture into explicit algebraic formulas.
We then investigate how to select elliptic curves suitable for cryptography, presenting the theorems of Hasse, Lenstra, and Schoof for curve sampling and point counting.
Next, we examine torsion subgroups and characterize the algebraic group structure of \(E(\mathbb{F}_p)\).
Finally, we study bilinear pairings on elliptic curves, exploring their role in discrete logarithm attacks as well as constructive cryptographic protocols.

\section*{The Algebraic Group Law}

Let \(K\) be a field whose characteristic is neither \(2\) nor \(3\).
An elliptic curve \(E\) over \(K\) in Weierstrass form is defined by
\[
    E \colon y^2 = x^3 + Ax + B, \quad \text{where } A, B \in K \text{ and } 4A^3 + 27B^2 \neq 0.
\]
We denote by \(E(K)\) the set of points \((x, y) \in K^2\) satisfying this equation, together with the point at infinity \(\infty\), which serves as the identity element.

\begin{definition}[Algebraic Group Operation]\label{def:algebraic-group-op}
    Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve defined over \(K\).
    For any two points \(P, Q \in E(K)\), their sum \(P + Q\) is defined as follows:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item If \(P = \infty\), then \(P + Q = Q\). If \(Q = \infty\), then \(P + Q = P\).
        \item If \(P = (x_1, y_1)\) and \(Q = (x_2, y_2)\) with \(x_1 = x_2\) and \(y_1 = -y_2\), then \(P + Q = \infty\).
        \item Otherwise, \(P + Q = (x_3, y_3)\), where
        \begin{align*}
            m &=
            \begin{cases}
                (y_2 - y_1) / (x_2 - x_1) & \text{if } P \neq Q, \\
                (3x_1^2 + A) / 2y_1 & \text{if } P = Q,
            \end{cases} \\
            x_3 &= m^2 - x_1 - x_2, \\
            y_3 &= m(x_1 - x_3) - y_1.
        \end{align*}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\subsection*{Derivation of the Group Law Formulas}

To see how these algebraic equations arise from geometry, consider the line \(\ell\) passing through \(P = (x_1, y_1)\) and \(Q = (x_2, y_2)\).
If \(P \neq Q\) and \(x_1 \neq x_2\), the line is the secant line through \(P\) and \(Q\), with slope \(m = (y_2 - y_1) / (x_2 - x_1)\).
If \(P = Q\) and \(y_1 \neq 0\), the line is the tangent to the curve at \(P\).
Implicitly differentiating \(y^2 = x^3 + Ax + B\) with respect to \(x\) yields
\[
    2y \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = 3x^2 + A \implies m = \frac{\mathrm{d}y}{\mathrm{d}x} = \frac{3x_1^2 + A}{2y_1}.
\]

The equation of \(\ell\) is \(y = m(x - x_1) + y_1\).
To find the intersection of \(\ell\) with the curve \(y^2 = x^3 + Ax + B\), we substitute \(y\) into the curve equation
\[
    \bigl(m(x - x_1) + y_1\bigr)^2 = x^3 + Ax + B.
\]
Expanding and rearranging into standard polynomial form gives
\[
    x^3 - m^2 x^2 + \bigl(A - 2m(y_1 - m x_1)\bigr)x + \bigl(B - (y_1 - m x_1)^2\bigr) = 0.
\]
This is a monic cubic polynomial whose roots are the \(x\)-coordinates of the three intersection points \(P\), \(Q\), and the third intersection point \(-R = (x_3, y_3')\).
Factoring as \((x - x_1)(x - x_2)(x - x_3) = 0\), the coefficient of \(x^2\) is \(-(x_1 + x_2 + x_3)\).
Equating this with the \(x^2\)-coefficient \(-m^2\) gives
\[
    -(x_1 + x_2 + x_3) = -m^2 \implies x_3 = m^2 - x_1 - x_2.
\]
The \(y\)-coordinate of the third intersection point on the line is \(y_3' = m(x_3 - x_1) + y_1\).
By the geometric definition, the sum \(P + Q\) is the reflection of this third intersection point across the \(x\)-axis:
\[
    y_3 = -y_3' = -\bigl(m(x_3 - x_1) + y_1\bigr) = m(x_1 - x_3) - y_1.
\]

\section*{Selecting Elliptic Curves for Cryptography}

To instantiate Diffie--Hellman key exchange over an elliptic curve group \(E(\mathbb{F}_p)\), we must choose the prime \(p\) and curve coefficients \(A, B \in \mathbb{F}_p\) such that the discrete logarithm problem is computationally infeasible.
By the Pohlig--Hellman algorithm, the group order \(N = \#E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) must not be smooth; ideally, \(N\) should be prime, or a small cofactor times a large prime.

\subsection*{Bounding and Counting Curve Points}

How many points can an elliptic curve have over a finite field \(\mathbb{F}_p\)?
Heuristically, for each \(x \in \mathbb{F}_p\), the value \(x^3 + Ax + B\) is a quadratic residue with probability \(\approx 1/2\) (yielding two points), zero with probability \(1/p\) (yielding one point), and a non-residue with probability \(\approx 1/2\) (yielding no points).
Including \(\infty\), the expected number of points is \(p + 1\).
Hasse formalized this intuition with a strict bound.

\begin{theorem}[Hasse's Theorem]\label{thm:hasse}
    Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve defined over \(\mathbb{F}_p\). The number of \(\mathbb{F}_p\)-rational points satisfies
    \[
        \bigl| \#E(\mathbb{F}_p) - (p + 1) \bigr| \leq 2\sqrt{p}.
    \]
\end{theorem}

The interval \([p + 1 - 2\sqrt{p}, p + 1 + 2\sqrt{p}]\) is known as the \emph{Hasse interval}.
To find a curve with prime order, we can choose random parameters \(A, B \in \mathbb{F}_p\) and check if \(\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) is prime.
The feasibility of this approach depends on the distribution of curve orders.

\begin{theorem}[Lenstra~\cite{Lenstra1987}]\label{thm:lenstra}
    Let \(A, B \in \mathbb{F}_p\) be chosen uniformly at random subject to \(4A^3 + 27B^2 \not\equiv 0 \pmod{p}\).
    Then \(\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) is distributed roughly uniformly across the Hasse interval.
\end{theorem}

By the Prime Number Theorem, a random integer of magnitude \(\approx p\) is prime with probability approximately \(\frac{1}{\ln p}\).
Hence, repeatedly sampling random parameters \(A, B\) yields a curve of prime order after \(O(\log p)\) trials on average.

To test candidates, we need an efficient method to compute \(\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)\).

\begin{theorem}[Schoof~\cite{Schoof1985}]\label{thm:schoof}
    There exists a deterministic algorithm that computes \(\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) in \(O((\log p)^8)\) bit operations.
\end{theorem}

\subsection*{A Cautionary Shortcut: Supersingular Curves}

Because point counting via Schoof's algorithm can be computationally intensive, one might consider choosing curves whose order is known without counting.
For instance, suppose we choose a prime \(p\) such that \(p + 1 = 2p'\) for some large prime \(p'\), and consider the curve
\[
    E \colon y^2 = x^3 + B \quad (\text{with } A = 0).
\]
When \(p \equiv 2 \pmod{3}\), every element in \(\mathbb{F}_p\) is a unique cube (the map \(x \mapsto x^3\) is an automorphism of \(\mathbb{F}_p^\times\)).
As \(x\) ranges over \(\mathbb{F}_p\), the polynomial \(x^3 + B\) assumes each value in \(\mathbb{F}_p\) exactly once:
it takes the value \(0\) once (contributing 1 point \((x, 0)\)), quadratic residues \((p-1)/2\) times (contributing \(2 \cdot (p-1)/2 = p-1\) points), and quadratic non-residues \((p-1)/2\) times (contributing 0 points).
Together with \(\infty\), the total number of points is
\[
    \#E(\mathbb{F}_p) = 1 + (p - 1) + 1 = p + 1 = 2p'.
\]
A similar phenomenon occurs for \(y^2 = x^3 + Ax\) (with \(B = 0\)) when \(p \equiv 3 \pmod{4}\), which also has \(\#E(\mathbb{F}_p) = p + 1\).
Such curves are called \emph{supersingular}.

This construction appears attractive: it immediately produces a subgroup of large prime order \(p'\) without running point counting algorithms.
However, as we will see shortly, this choice is completely insecure: the condition \(p' \mid (p + 1)\) allows pairings to reduce the discrete logarithm problem to a small extension field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}^\times\), where subexponential index calculus attacks break the system.

\section*{Torsion Subgroups and Group Structure}

\begin{definition}[Torsion Subgroup]\label{def:torsion}
    Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over a field \(K\). For any integer \(n \geq 1\), the \emph{\(n\)-torsion subgroup} of \(E(K)\) is
    \[
        E(K)[n] = \{ P \in E(K) : nP = \infty \}.
    \]
\end{definition}

\begin{example}[Two-Torsion Points]
    For \(n = 2\), a point \(P = (x, y) \in E(K)\) satisfies \(2P = \infty\) if and only if \(P = -P = (x, -y)\), which occurs if and only if \(y = 0\).
    Substituting \(y = 0\) into \(y^2 = x^3 + Ax + B\), the affine \(2\)-torsion points correspond to roots of the cubic polynomial
    \[
        x^3 + Ax + B = 0.
    \]

    If the polynomial has no roots in \(K\), then \(E(K)[2] = \{ \infty \} \cong \{0\}\).
    If the polynomial has exactly one root \(x_1 \in K\), then \(E(K)[2] = \{ \infty, (x_1, 0) \} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2\).
    If the polynomial splits completely into three distinct roots \(x_1, x_2, x_3 \in K\), then
    \[
        E(K)[2] = \{ \infty, (x_1, 0), (x_2, 0), (x_3, 0) \} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2.
    \]
\end{example}

Notice that over an algebraically closed field \(\overline{K}\) (where every non-constant polynomial factors completely into linear factors), the cubic always has three distinct roots (since \(4A^3 + 27B^2 \neq 0\)).
Thus, \(E(\overline{K})[2] \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_2\).
This two-dimensional structure generalizes to all torsion orders coprime to the field characteristic.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:full-torsion}
    Let \(p\) be a prime, and let \(n \geq 1\) be an integer coprime to \(p\).
    Over the algebraic closure \(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p\),
    \[
        E(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p)[n] \cong \mathbb{Z}_n \times \mathbb{Z}_n.
    \]
\end{theorem}

The proof of \Cref{thm:full-torsion} is beyond the scope of this course.

\begin{corollary}[Group Structure of Elliptic Curves over Finite Fields]\label{cor:ec-group-structure}
    Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve defined over \(\mathbb{F}_p\). Then there exist positive integers \(m_1, m_2\) such that
    \[
        E(\mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}_{m_1} \times \mathbb{Z}_{m_2}.
    \]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
    Let \(n = \#E(\mathbb{F}_p)\).
    If \(p \nmid n\), then by Lagrange's Theorem, \(nP = \infty\) for every \(P \in E(\mathbb{F}_p)\).
    Therefore,
    \[
        E(\mathbb{F}_p) \subseteq E(\overline{\mathbb{F}}_p)[n] \cong \mathbb{Z}_n \times \mathbb{Z}_n.
    \]
    Any subgroup of \(\mathbb{Z}_n \times \mathbb{Z}_n\) is generated by at most two elements, and is therefore isomorphic to \(\mathbb{Z}_{m_1} \times \mathbb{Z}_{m_2}\).
    If \(p \mid n\), Hasse's bound implies \(|n - (p + 1)| \leq 2\sqrt{p} < p\) for all \(p \geq 5\), forcing \(n = p\), which is cyclic: \(E(\mathbb{F}_p) \cong \mathbb{Z}_p\).
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
    While \(E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) is isomorphic to \(\mathbb{Z}_{m_1} \times \mathbb{Z}_{m_2}\), this isomorphism is not efficiently computable.
    If an efficient isomorphism to \(\mathbb{Z}_{m_1} \times \mathbb{Z}_{m_2}\) were known, the discrete logarithm problem on \(E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) would reduce to simple division in \(\mathbb{Z}_{m_2}\), breaking all elliptic curve cryptography.
\end{remark}

\section*{Bilinear Pairings}

Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(\mathbb{F}_p\) and let \(n\) be an integer coprime to \(p\).
Let \(\mu_n = \{ \zeta \in \overline{\mathbb{F}}_p^\times : \zeta^n = 1 \}\) denote the group of \(n\)-th roots of unity, and let \(k \geq 1\) be the smallest integer such that \(\mu_n \subseteq \mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times\).

\begin{definition}[Pairing]\label{def:pairing}
    A \emph{pairing} is a non-degenerate bilinear map
    \[
        e \colon E[n] \times E[n] \to \mu_n \subseteq \mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times.
    \]
\end{definition}

Bilinearity means that, for all \(P, Q, R \in E[n]\),
\[
    e(P + Q, R) = e(P, R) \cdot e(Q, R) \quad \text{and} \quad e(P, Q + R) = e(P, Q) \cdot e(P, R).
\]
In particular, for any integers \(a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\),
\[
    e(aP, bQ) = e(P, Q)^{ab}.
\]
Non-degeneracy means that if \(e(P, Q) = 1\) for all \(Q \in E[n]\), then \(P = \infty\); and if \(e(P, Q) = 1\) for all \(P \in E[n]\), then \(Q = \infty\).

We leave the construction of pairings to the next lecture, but we note that the Tate--Lichtenbaum pairing is a classical example of an efficiently computable pairing.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:embedding-degree}
    The smallest integer \(k\) such that \(\mu_n \subseteq \mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times\) is the smallest positive integer \(k\) satisfying
    \[
        n \mid (p^k - 1) \quad (\text{or equivalently, } p^k \equiv 1 \pmod{n}).
    \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
    The multiplicative group \(\mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times\) is cyclic of order \(p^k - 1\).
    A cyclic group of order \(M\) contains a subgroup of order \(n\) if and only if \(n \mid M\).
    Setting \(M = p^k - 1\) yields the result.
\end{proof}

\section*{Applications and Implications of Pairings}

Bilinear pairings are a double-edged sword: they enable powerful cryptographic protocols that cannot be realized in ordinary cyclic groups, but they also provide a venue for attacks that break curves with unsuitable parameters.

\subsection*{Application 1: Checking Equality of Discrete Logarithms}

Given two discrete logarithm instances \(P_1, Q_1 = x_1 P_1\) and \(P_2, Q_2 = x_2 P_2\), we can verify whether \(x_1 = x_2\) without computing either discrete logarithm.
By bilinearity,
\begin{align*}
    e(P_1, Q_2) &= e(P_1, x_2 P_2) = e(P_1, P_2)^{x_2}, \\
    e(Q_1, P_2) &= e(x_1 P_1, P_2) = e(P_1, P_2)^{x_1}.
\end{align*}
Assuming that \(e(P_1, P_2)\) has order \(n\), equality \(e(P_1, Q_2) = e(Q_1, P_2)\) holds if and only if \(x_1 \equiv x_2 \pmod{n}\).

In particular, setting \(P_1 = P\), \(Q_1 = aP\), \(P_2 = bP\), and \(Q_2 = C\), this checks whether \(C = abP\), which shows that the Decisional Diffie--Hellman (DDH) problem is computationally easy in groups with an efficient pairing.
Groups in which DDH is easy while the Computational Diffie--Hellman (CDH) problem is believed to remain hard are known as \emph{Gap Diffie--Hellman (GDH) groups}.

\subsection*{Application 2: Tripartite One-Round Diffie--Hellman}

In standard Diffie--Hellman, two parties establish a shared secret in a single round of communication.
Extending this to three parties typically requires multiple rounds.
Joux~\cite{Joux2004} showed that bilinear pairings allow three parties (Alice, Bob, and Charlie) to establish a shared secret in a single round:

A point \(P \in E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) of prime order \(n\), together with a pairing \(e\) for which \(e(P, P)\) has order \(n\), is publicly known. Alice, Bob, and Charlie select private keys \(a, b, c \in \mathbb{Z}_n\) and compute their respective public points \(A = aP\), \(B = bP\), and \(C = cP\). They broadcast these public points to one another in a single round. Alice then computes \(K_A = e(B, C)^a\), Bob computes \(K_B = e(A, C)^b\), and Charlie computes \(K_C = e(A, B)^c\). By bilinearity,
\begin{align*}
    K_A &= e(B, C)^a = e(bP, cP)^a = e(P, P)^{abc}, \\
    K_B &= e(A, C)^b = e(aP, cP)^b = e(P, P)^{abc}, \\
    K_C &= e(A, B)^c = e(aP, bP)^c = e(P, P)^{abc}.
\end{align*}
Thus, all three parties arrive at the identical shared key \(K = e(P, P)^{abc} \in \mu_n \subseteq \mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times\) in a single round.

\subsection*{Application 3: Subexponential Discrete Logarithm Attacks}

Pairings can also be used as a cryptanalytic reduction.
The \emph{Menezes--Okamoto\---Vanstone (MOV) attack}~\cite{MenezesOkamotoVanstone1993} reduces the discrete logarithm problem in the elliptic curve group \(E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) to the discrete logarithm problem in the multiplicative group of the extension field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times\).

Suppose an attacker is given \(P \in E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) of prime order \(n\) and \(Q = xP\), and seeks to find \(x \in \mathbb{Z}_n\).
Using a pairing \(e\), the attacker computes
\[
    u = e(P, P) \in \mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times \quad \text{and} \quad v = e(P, Q) = e(P, xP) = e(P, P)^x = u^x \in \mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times.
\]
The relation \(u^x = v\) is an instance of the discrete logarithm problem in the finite field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times\).
While generic algorithms on \(E(\mathbb{F}_p)\) take exponential time \(O(\sqrt{n})\), discrete logarithms in \(\mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times\) can be solved in \emph{subexponential time} via index calculus, with complexity
\[
    L_{p^k}\left[1/3, \, c\right] = \exp\left( \bigl(c + o(1)\bigr) (\ln p^k)^{1/3} (\ln \ln p^k)^{2/3} \right).
\]

For this reduction to not compromise security, we need \(k\) to be sufficiently large so that discrete logarithms in \(\mathbb{F}_{p^k}^\times\) remain intractable.
This explains why the earlier shortcut of choosing \(A = 0\) with \(p + 1 = 2p'\) fails: because \(n = p' \mid (p + 1) \mid (p^2 - 1)\), the curve has \(k = 2\), reducing the problem to a \(512\)-bit field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^2}^\times\) that is readily broken by index calculus.
For standard curves, \(k\) is typically on the order of \(n\) itself (\(k \approx 2^{256}\)), making the reduction harmless.
At the same time, for constructive pairing-based protocols (such as tripartite Diffie--Hellman), we want \(k\) to be small enough so that pairings are efficiently computable, while ensuring that \(p^k\) is large enough (e.g., \(\approx 3072\) bits) to withstand subexponential attacks.
